Who Wears the Future?

How age, lifestyle and geography shape wearable technology adoption.

From tracking our daily steps to monitoring our heart rate, technology is becoming part of what we wear every day. Smartwatches and fitness trackers are no longer just accessories; they can support our health, fitness and daily routines. But how widely are these devices being adopted, and who is using them? Through 12 interactive visualisations, this story explores the companies shaping the industry, where ownership is strongest, how adoption differs across population groups, and what people find most useful about these devices.

1 - The Global Wearable Market

Before exploring who wears these devices, it’s worth understanding the companies behind them. The global market reveals which manufacturers lead and how much of the industry exists beyond its biggest names.

Leading wearable companies

Q1 2025–Q1 2026 · share of worldwide wearable-device shipments

Insight: Apple leads, but its position fluctuates

Apple maintained the highest individual shipment share among the five companies shown, reaching a peak of 20.9% in Q4 2025. However, its share fell to 18.1% in the following quarter, while Huawei regained ground.

This shows that even established market leaders experience changes in their competitive position, with other manufacturers continuing to challenge them.

Source: IDC Quarterly Wearable Device Tracker, 2026 Q1 ↗

How is the wearable market divided?

Q1 2026 · percentage of worldwide units shipped, including “Others”

Insight: The market extends beyond its biggest names

Although Apple leads individually, it accounted for only 18.1% of worldwide device shipments in Q1 2026. In comparison, companies outside the top five collectively represented 48%.

This puts Apple's leadership into perspective. Almost half of global shipments come from manufacturers beyond the five largest companies, revealing a much broader industry than the leading names alone suggest.

Source: IDC Quarterly Wearable Device Tracker, 2026 Q1 ↗

2 - A World of Wearables

We know which companies supply these devices, but where are people actually using them? Looking at ownership across countries reveals differences in the popularity of smartwatches and fitness-focused wristbands.

Where are smartwatches most popular?

Q2 2025 · percent of surveyed internet users aged 16+ who own a smartwatch

Insight: Ownership crosses borders

Croatia, the UAE and Norway recorded the highest smartwatch ownership rates among the surveyed markets, all exceeding 38%.

Their positions across different regions show that smartwatch adoption is not concentrated in one part of the world. However, the variation in shading across the map also reveals considerable differences in ownership between countries.

Source: GWI / Kepios, Digital 2026 report, p. 232 ↗

Where are smart wristbands popular?

Nine selected countries · Q2 2025 · circle area = wristband ownership (%)

Insight: Fitness bands follow a different pattern

Only nine deliberately selected markets are shown; circle positions are approximate country locations.

China stands out for its particularly high smart wristband ownership, with Spain and Poland also recording relatively high rates among the selected countries.

Unlike smartwatches, fitness bands are generally designed around activity and health tracking. Their different geographical pattern suggests that the popularity of one type of wrist-worn technology doesn't necessarily reflect the popularity of another.

Source: GWI / Kepios, Digital 2026 report, p. 235 ↗

3 - Australia on the Wrist

Bringing the story closer to home, how are Australians embracing this technology? Changes across age groups, gender and location reveal who is adopting connected devices and where differences remain.

Older Australians are catching up

Australians aged 55+ · percentage owning a connected wearable, 2023–2024

Insight: Older generations are embracing connected technology

Only adults aged 55+ are shown here; this is not the all-Australian ownership rate.

Among Australians aged 55 and over, connected-wearable ownership increased across every age group shown between 2023 and 2024. The largest rise occurred among adults aged 75 and over, from 13% to 23%.

This suggests that adoption is extending further into older generations, demonstrating that connected technology is becoming more common beyond traditionally younger users.

Source: ACMA, How we use the internet (2024, 2025) ↗

Who is more likely to own one?

Australian adults · connected wearable ownership by gender and location, 2025

Insight: Ownership differences are relatively modest

Australian women reported slightly higher connected-wearable ownership than men, while metropolitan residents also recorded higher rates than regional residents.

However, these differences were only 6 and 5 percentage points respectively. Gender and location are associated with ownership, but neither creates an especially large divide in the figures shown.

Source: ACMA, 2025 summary, section 5 ↗

4 - Who Wears Them?

Beyond geography, individual characteristics may also be associated with technology adoption. Age, gender, education and employment help reveal which groups are more likely to own these devices.

Smartwatch ownership across age and gender

GWI Q2 2025 · surveyed internet users aged 16+; darker blue = greater ownership

Insight: Younger users lead smartwatch adoption

Internet users aged 16+ in GWI surveyed markets; limited 65+ country coverage.

Ownership is generally higher among younger surveyed internet users, with women aged 16–24 reporting 31.9% ownership compared with 27.4% among men of the same age.

The heatmap also shows that gender differences vary across age groups. Age is an important pattern in smartwatch adoption, but the relationship between gender and ownership is less consistent.

Source: GWI / Kepios, Digital 2026 report, p. 233 ↗

Which demographic gaps are widest?

Duke Health respondents, US, January 2022 · difference within pairs (percentage points)

Insight: Age shows the largest ownership gap

January 2022 Duke Health patient advisory sample; not population-representative.

In the Duke Health survey, age produced the largest difference among the demographic comparisons shown. Ownership reached 71% among respondents aged 18–25, compared with 39% among those aged 77 and over.

Education and employment were also associated with ownership, but their displayed gaps were smaller. This highlights age as a particularly important factor within the surveyed group, not necessarily across the entire population.

Source: npj Digital Medicine (2024), Duke Health sample ↗

5 - Beyond Telling Time

Owning a device is only part of the story. What makes people use them, which features do they value, and what frustrations remain? Survey responses offer a closer look at how tracking technology fits into everyday life.

Which features matter most?

Usefulness ranking · separate 2016 and 2023 surveys · hover for percentages

Insight: Health monitoring takes the lead

2016: Australian survey. 2023: different international sample.

Step counting was the highest-rated feature for usefulness in the 2016 survey, while heart-rate monitoring ranked first in 2023.

This difference highlights the range of features people may value beyond basic activity tracking. Monitoring personal health appears particularly important in the more recent survey, although different participant groups mean this is not a direct change in the same users' preferences.

Source: Beckett et al., JMIR (2025), Table 3 ↗

Why do people wear trackers?

2023 survey · respondents could select multiple motivations

Insight: Personal health is a leading motivation

Improving health was the most frequently selected motivation in the 2023 survey, chosen by 50.3% of respondents, followed closely by monitoring daily activities.

The value of fitness trackers extends beyond collecting data. For many surveyed users, the appeal lies in using that information to better understand their habits and support personal health goals.

Source: Beckett et al., JMIR (2025), Table 2 ↗

Where wearable devices fall short

Reported complaints in separate 2016 and 2023 surveys

Insight: Everyday frustrations still matter

The 2016 and 2023 results come from separate respondent groups.

Although having no complaints was the most common individual response in 2023, battery life and technical issues remained prominent concerns.

Even when tracking technology offers useful features, the everyday experience of wearing and maintaining a device still matters. Reliability and convenience remain important considerations alongside functionality.

Source: Beckett et al., JMIR (2025), Table 6 ↗

The Future on Our Wrists

From global manufacturers to everyday users, wearable technology is shaped by much more than the devices themselves. The industry extends beyond its leading brands, while ownership patterns vary across countries, age groups and lifestyles.

In Australia, connected devices are becoming more common among older generations, and international research shows that people often value the health and activity insights these devices provide. Yet greater functionality doesn't automatically mean a better experience. Battery life, technical reliability and ease of use remain important.

The future of wearable technology is therefore not simply about what these devices can measure, but how effectively they fit into people's lives. As technology develops, wider adoption will depend on features that people find useful, accessible and convenient.

These visualisations draw on different industry and survey sources. Their percentages describe different populations and measures, so they should not be combined into one global ownership rate.
